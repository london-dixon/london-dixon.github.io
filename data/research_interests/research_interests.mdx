## Lexical Nonselectivity in Bilinguals
My first line of research focuses on nonselectivity within the bilingual lexicon and how
script differences influence L1 and L2 activation during online comprehension. My second line of research focuses on how Chinese-English bilinguals map compound words in Chinese to mono-morphemic and multi-morphemic words in English. I'm interested in not only how whole translations are represented in the lexicons but also how individual constituents are connected. My qualifying paper was on how Chinese-English bilinguals mapped compound words in Chinese to mono-morphemic words in English. I found that Chinese-English bilinguals showed evidence of accessing L1 translation equivalents in accuracy data, suggesting some evidence of accessing L1
translation equivalents during online L2 processing. Additionally, I found ualitatively different semantic priming results based on language proficiency. 

I'm currently working on a project looking at how Chinese-English bilinguals mapped compound words in Chinese to compound words in English. To do this, I'm using a picture-word interference task. 

Previously, I have collaborated with other colleagues on researching prediction in Chinese-English and Sinhala-English bilinguals during continuous auditory processing (Thorburn, Karunathilake, Dixon, et al., in prep). Using MEG, we found that late learners of English leverage prediction in similar ways to native speakers at sub-lexical, lexical and sentential levels of representation.

## Simulating Cross-linguistic Semantic and Morphological Priming
Using the [Multilink model](https://multilink.donders.ru.nl/), a localist-connectionist model of bilingual visual word processing, I have been working with [Dr. Naomi Feldman](https://users.umiacs.umd.edu/~nhf/) to investigate how words are connected across the lexicons in cross-script bilinguals. Our primary goal is to simulate the influence of cross-linguistic priming variables on lexical decision performance in Chinese-English bilinguals.Secondarily, we are interested in how proficiency influences recognition.

## Science Communication
I'm a firm believer in engaging with the public about language science. I got my first taste of language science public outreach when I was a research fellow at The Ohio State in the summer of 2018. While there, I worked with [Dr. Monique Mills](https://www.uh.edu/class/comd/people/faculty/monique-t-mills/), [Dr. Laura Wagner](https://psychology.osu.edu/people/wagner.602), [Dr. Kathryn Campbell-Kibler](https://linguistics.osu.edu/people/campbellkibler.1), and [Dr. Nikole Patson](https://psychology.osu.edu/people/patson.3). 

Since starting my PhD at UMD, I've been a member of the [Language Science Center](https://languagescience.umd.edu/) and volunteered with their outreach team. I also joined the [Language Science Station at Planet Word](https://sites.google.com/umd.edu/planetwordresearch), a language museum in Washington D.C. The Language Science Station is a multi-university partnership, which trains students who are underrepresented in the language science community and introduces language science to the public through demonstrations and research. As a part of the Language Science Station, I’ve been able to not only mentor other students from various universities but also continue to introduce language science to the public.

In the summer of 2026, I was able to introduce more students to science communication and public engagement while teaching *Science Communication and Language Science at the Museum*. This course is designed to give hands-on experience in science communication, focusing specifically on communicating about language science, or the study of how language is learned, used, and represented in the mind. Students were given the opportunity to develop their science communication skills while interacting with visitors at Planet Word.
